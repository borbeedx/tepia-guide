Before You Enter
This is a working
session, not a field trip.

TEPIA is where you gather real observations that feed directly into what your team builds. Here's how to make the most of it.

Before the visit

Arrive with a question already in your head. What about this technology is relevant to the robot my team is designing? What am I curious about? What might surprise me?

During the visit

Observe. Write things down — not just what the host says, but what you notice, what's missing, what doesn't match what you expected.

Photographs are fine but they are not observations. Words are observations.

After the visit

Bring at least one observation to the next standup. Something you saw, something that changed your thinking, something you now want to ask. The observations from visits feed directly into what your team builds.

💡 The Most Useful Observations

The most useful observations are usually the small ones. Not "this exhibit is impressive" — but "I noticed this technology was designed for people who can't use touchscreens." Small details tell you what's really happening.

Exhibition Guide
All 8 Zones — Explained

All exhibits at TEPIA are displayed in Japanese. This guide gives you the English summary of each zone and exhibit so you can follow along and understand what you're seeing.

Zone 1 · 1F Entrance
Prologue Area — The World's Big Problems
▾

This is the first zone you enter. It sets the stage for everything you'll see inside — using a giant mural, animated displays, and video messages from real innovators.

Learning Objectives
  • Understand that today's social issues (aging, climate change, waste, inequality) need technology AND human cooperation to solve
  • See how young people and established innovators both have a role in building the future
  • Connect the idea of "social issues" to something personally relevant to you
  • Hear directly from technology leaders about why they chose their fields
CONTEXT
Issues Abound in Our World Today

A display introducing global-scale problems like climate change and overpopulation, alongside everyday problems like waste disposal. These "convenient lifestyle byproducts" often require the whole world to cooperate on solutions. The exhibit asks: What sorts of issues affect your way of life?

VIDEO
Messages from Leading Innovators

Maki Komuro (Switch Education) · Daiyuu Nobori (IPA Cyber Security) · Ryuma Niiyama (Meiji University, Soft Robotics)

Video messages from three technology leaders who each chose a different path — programming education, cybersecurity, and robots that feel like human skin. Pay attention to why they entered their field.

Zone 2 · Showcase Area
Young Innovators — Students Who Built Something Real
▾

This is the most relevant zone for you. It features projects created by Japanese students — people your age — who saw a real problem and built something to solve it. Their work is exhibited here alongside professional researchers.

Learning Objectives
  • See that young people can and do build real, deployable technology
  • Understand how problem-first thinking (not technology-first) drives good innovation
  • Notice how each project connects a personal experience to a broader social issue
  • Draw parallels to your own robot project — what problem are you solving, and for whom?
PROJECT 1
Nolack — Medicine + Technology Platform

Rei Otsuka · Shibuya Kyoiku Gakuen Shibuya High School

Built during COVID-19 when masks were scarce. Nolack is a web platform connecting people who need medical supplies with those who can donate them. Future vision includes smart glasses with AI to display key info automatically. Key idea: Broaden your perspective beyond your personal view to provide the best solutions.

PROJECT 2
FraudShield AI — Phone Scam Detection

Ikuma Naba · Sotetsu Nishitani · Fumiya Taira · Tokyo Metropolitan College of Industrial Technology, Shinagawa Campus

After Nishitani was personally defrauded, the team built an AI that listens to phone calls in real time, detects fraud patterns using deep learning, and warns the caller instantly. Detection rate: over 90%. Won the grand prize at DCON2024.

PROJECT 3
KOSEN Robo-Con — Fruit-Picking Robot

Daichi Shimotsu · Rikuo Takada · National Institute of Technology (KOSEN), Wakayama College

Built "Gouki Kadan" for the 2023 national robotics competition. The robot climbs wooden planks like a tank, picks fruit-shaped balls, and breaks through obstacles at high speed. Split responsibilities: one student handled mechanics, the other circuitry. "We learned skills at the robotics club that we couldn't have learned in a classroom."

PROJECT 4
Social Problem-Solving with Self-Made Robots

Noi Tatsuzaki · Leave a Nest Co., Ltd.

Joined the robotics team SAKURA Tempesta in first year of junior high school. Youngest member, in charge of most designs. Qualified for world championships four times. Also donated 2,000 face shields during COVID. Goal: "Become an engineer who looks at society from an engineer's point of view, identifies problems, and takes action."

Zone 3 · Showcase — Living & Economy
Technologies Improving Daily Life
▾

Technologies addressing Japan's most pressing everyday challenges: aging population, healthcare gaps, and the need for more human-centered interfaces.

Learning Objectives
  • See how "Living & Economy" problems translate directly into engineering challenges
  • Notice how each technology starts from a specific human inconvenience or pain point
  • Consider how your robot might integrate with or complement one of these technologies
A1
Palm-Size Thermoelectric Module

National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS)

A small, clean power device made from common elements (iron, aluminum, silicon) that generates electricity from the temperature difference between your hand and the module. No external power needed — it's ecological, cheap, and harmless. Designed to power the billions of sensors needed for a "smart society."

A2
xpression camera — AI Expression-Changing App

EmbodyMe, Inc.

An app that uses AI to map your facial expressions onto a virtual avatar in real time for video calls. Addresses "virtual meeting fatigue" — the stress of always being on camera. One photo of your face is all you need. Future version: control avatar expressions by voice or text only, no camera needed.

A3
Aerial Display with ASKA3D-Plate — Holographic Touch Panel

Asukanet Co., Ltd.

Uses light reflection to display images floating in mid-air. Operated without touching anything — just infrared sensing. You can wear gloves and still use it. Important for hygiene in public spaces. Viewing angle limits who can see the display — a built-in privacy feature.

♿
Zone 4 · Showcase — Society
Technologies for Inclusion & Accessibility
▾

Technologies designed to make society more inclusive — for people with visual challenges, elderly citizens, and children building daily habits.

Learning Objectives
  • Understand "inclusive design" — building technology so more people can use it, not just the typical user
  • See how the same material (like fabric or sound) can be redesigned for completely new purposes
  • Think about who your robot is designed for, and who might be excluded
B1
Fabric Speaker — Fabric that Outputs Sound

National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (AIST)

A conductive fabric with silver-plated fibers that acts like a speaker. Light, soft, stretchy — can be sewn into clothing, car seats, or beds. Delivers sound and information without screens. Designed for people who need ambient, stress-free audio information.

B2
Dynaglass — Wearable AI Voice Guide for the Visually Impaired

Digitalattendant Co., Ltd.

Glasses fitted with cameras and AI that reads text aloud AND speaks to warn you about traffic signals, people, objects, and dangers ahead. Works alongside a guide dog or white cane — it doesn't replace them, it adds information. The goal: help people with visual challenges go out and live more safely.

B3
Keipu-Sb — Rear-Entry Mobility Device

AIZUK Inc.

A new type of wheelchair where the user enters from the back — eliminating the risky transfer process. Seat height adjusts with a button. Rotates 360° on the spot for tight spaces like elevators. Initially developed for hospital workforce shortages (one caregiver can manage alone). Now targeting art galleries, museums, and airports too. Next version: autonomous driving to guide users to destinations.

Zone 5 · Workshop Area
Humans and AI — Understanding & Creating with AI
▾

This is the most hands-on zone. It covers what AI is, how it learns, and lets you actually co-create with AI tools built by student developers.

Learning Objectives
  • Understand the difference between AI, machine learning, neural networks, and deep learning
  • See that AI gets smarter through data and feedback — not magic
  • Experience what "human-AI collaboration" feels like in practice
  • Recognise that the apps here were built by people your age — and you could build something similar
ROOM 1
Is It Impossible to Live Without AI?

An immersive display showing all the places AI is already active — often without you knowing. Most people use AI daily without being aware of it.

ROOM 2
What Is AI? — Japanese Researchers' Perspectives

Even among researchers, there is no single clear definition of AI. This display shows how different Japanese AI researchers think about the question — and why that ambiguity matters.

ROOM 3
How Does AI Get Smarter?

Explanatory videos on machine learning, neural networks, and deep learning. AI gains abilities through learning, just like people — it can't do everything from the start.

C1
Writing Assistant App

Developer: Komako Takahashi

An AI app for writing essays and book reports — built by a student. Designed to make writing less intimidating and more enjoyable.

C2
AI 4-Panel Comic Maker

Developer: Kai Tanaka

Draw something, and the AI turns it into a 4-panel comic with dialogue. Combines creative input with AI output.

C3
Future Sound Lab

Developer: Itsuki Tsuruta

Choose songs you like, and the AI learns your taste to compose music you'll love. The more input you give it, the better it gets — demonstrating feedback loops in machine learning.

ROOM 5
Things to Know About AI — The Risks

A society with AI isn't automatically good. This room asks: What kinds of problems might arise? Bias, misuse, over-reliance — this exhibit challenges you to think critically, not just enthusiastically.

ROOM 6
What Is Your Future With AI?

AI will keep advancing. This room asks you to think about ideas for how you personally can bring AI into daily life — and what role you want to play in shaping that future.

Zone 6 · Humans and Nature
Living in Harmony with Nature — Sustainability Technologies
▾

Technologies that help solve Japan's most pressing environmental challenges — using forests, oceans, and waste as resources instead of burdens.

Learning Objectives
  • Understand "upcycling" — turning waste into valuable material
  • See how biodegradable materials are being engineered at the molecular level
  • Think about the environmental footprint of your robot's components
D1
Technologies to Utilize Forest Resources

Osaka University · Kyoto University · Ministry of the Environment (NCV Project) · Daio Paper Corporation

Introduces technologies that transform Japan's abundant forest resources into innovative, carbon-neutral materials. All exhibitors focus on cellulose nanofiber — extracted from wood, it is lighter and stronger than steel, and fully biodegradable.

D2
Technologies to Utilize Marine Resources

Nippon Steel Corporation · Kaneka Corporation · Koushi Chemical Industry · Nichimo Co., Ltd.

Introduces technologies tackling mass disposal of surplus materials, coastal degradation, and marine plastic pollution. Includes: iron-based seaweed fertiliser from steel slag (Nippon Steel), biodegradable spoons and packaging (Kaneka), Hotamet — a helmet made from scallop shells (Koushi Chemical), and biodegradable ropes and octopus traps (Nichimo).

D3
Technologies to Utilize Waste

fabula Co., Ltd. · Takuma Co., Ltd.

Introduces technologies that find new value in things that would otherwise be discarded, contributing to local community solutions. fabula creates new materials from food waste; Takuma runs biomass power plants using vegetable cultivation via trigeneration.

Zone 7 · Special Exhibits
TEPIA Special Award Winners — Kids Design Awards
▾

TEPIA partners with Japan's Kids Design Awards to find the best designs that explain advanced technology to children. These are the winners — technologies designed for kids to understand and interact with.

Learning Objectives
  • Understand what makes a technology "kid-friendly" — transparent, tangible, safe
  • See how household robots and companion devices are being designed for behaviour change, not just convenience
  • Think about how your own robot might be designed to be understood by someone who's never seen it before
2025 AWARD
Pastel Sewing Machine

AXEYAMAZAKI Co., LTD.

A genuine sewing machine for children with a transparent structure so you can see exactly how it works as you use it. Threading and tension adjustment are simplified so kids can operate it without frustration. Motto: "Sew, Learn, Play." — encourages computational thinking by having kids plan, choose materials, and execute step by step.

2024 AWARD
BOCCO emo — Support for Children's Lifestyle Habits

Yukai Engineering · Tokyo Kasei University · Tokyo Gas Urban Life Research Institute

A cute robot that helps children develop daily routines. Exchanges voice messages with family, monitors schedules, and delivers helpful reminders twice a day (morning and evening) in a friendly voice. Messages are created with Tokyo Kasei University and energy-conservation expertise from Tokyo Gas.

2023 AWARD
Hobonichi Globe

Hobonichi Co., Ltd.

A spherical model of Earth using AR technology. Hold up a phone and the globe comes alive with real-time cloud cover, weather, animals, prehistoric dinosaurs, and world heritage sites. Constantly updated. More than 25 categories of geographic data.

🛠️
Zone 8 · 2F — Creative Lab & Workshop
Make Things, Program Robots
▾

The 2nd floor is where you make things. 3D printers, laser cutters, programming stations, and spherical robots to program — this is the hands-on maker space of TEPIA.

Learning Objectives
  • Experience the creative process of building — not just observing
  • See how "block-based programming" (like Scratch) is being used to teach robotics to beginners
  • Connect the making tools here to your own internship project — what could you prototype here?
2F LAB
Creative Lab

A space with 3D printers, laser cutters, and 3D CAD software. Seminars and workshops encourage kids to design and build. Online classes also available so students can participate from home. Items made here are displayed at the Lab Entrance on the way in.

WORKSHOP
Workshop Area — Program a Spherical Robot

Using a visual app with combinable command blocks, you program spherical robots to move around the floor while avoiding collisions. No coding experience needed — the blocks snap together like puzzle pieces. This is the same concept behind your robotics work at the internship.

GAME
Sankoro Bingo — Dementia Prevention e-Sport

Kanagawa Institute of Technology

A dice-shaped bingo game that combines brain training, physical activity, and social interaction — three factors known to help prevent dementia. Simple enough for elderly players, but fun for all ages. Widely used at community events across Japan.
